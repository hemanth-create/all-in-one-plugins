# Phrases: word- and phrase-level tells

Reworked from stop-slop's `phrases.md`. The catalog is kept because it is genuinely useful; the
framing is changed. These are *usual suspects*, not banned words. Cut them when they add no
information. Where a word can be load-bearing, a **Keep when** note says so.

## Throat-clearing openers

Announcements that a point is coming, standing in for the point. State the content directly.

- "Here's the thing:"
- "Here's what / this / that [X]"
- "Here's why [X]"
- "The uncomfortable truth is"
- "It turns out"
- "The real [X] is"
- "Let me be clear"
- "The truth is,"
- "I'll say it again:"
- "I'm going to be honest"
- "Can we talk about"
- "What I find interesting is"
- "Here's the problem though"

Any "here's what / this / that" construction is a run-up. Cut it and state the point.

**Keep when:** a genuine transition orients the reader before a shift — "First, the data:" or
"One caveat:" is signposting, not throat-clearing.

## Emphasis crutches

These add stress, not meaning. Usually delete.

- "Full stop." / "Period."
- "Let that sink in."
- "This matters because…"
- "Make no mistake"
- "Here's why that matters"

**Keep when:** "This matters because…" is immediately followed by a *specific, non-obvious* reason
the reader would otherwise miss. If the reason is obvious, cut the whole line.

## Business jargon

Replace with plain words.

| Avoid | Use instead |
|-------|-------------|
| Navigate (challenges) | Handle, address |
| Unpack (analysis) | Explain, examine |
| Lean into | Accept, commit to |
| Landscape (context) | Situation, field |
| Game-changer | Significant, important |
| Double down | Commit, increase |
| Deep dive | Analysis, examination |
| Take a step back | Reconsider |
| Moving forward | Next, from now on |
| Circle back | Return to, revisit |
| On the same page | Aligned, agreed |

## Adverbs and intensifiers

The original said "kill all adverbs." That over-corrects. Most `-ly` words and intensifiers are empty
emphasis propping up a weak verb or hedging a claim — cut those. But an adverb that carries
information stays.

Usual offenders (delete, then reread; if nothing was lost, they were filler):

- "really", "very", "just", "simply", "actually", "literally"
- "genuinely", "honestly", "truly", "deeply"
- "fundamentally", "inherently", "inevitably"
- "interestingly", "importantly", "crucially"

**The test:** does the word add information the sentence loses without it?

- Filler: "This is *really* hard." → "This is hard." (nothing lost)
- Load-bearing: "The test fails *intermittently*." (frequency is the fact)
- Load-bearing: "She replied in French, *fluently*." (the point of the sentence)
- Load-bearing: "*simply*" meaning *in one step, no configuration*; "*literally*" when you mean it.

Also usually-cuttable filler frames:

- "At its core"
- "In today's [X]"
- "It's worth noting"
- "At the end of the day"
- "When it comes to"
- "In a world where"
- "The reality is"

## Meta-commentary

Self-referential asides about the writing's own structure. The piece should move, not narrate itself.

- "Hint:", "Plot twist:", "Spoiler:"
- "You already know this, but"
- "But that's another post"
- "X is a feature, not a bug"
- "The rest of this essay explains…"
- "Let me walk you through…"
- "In this section, we'll…"
- "As we'll see…"
- "I want to explore…"

**Keep when:** in technical docs, an explicit roadmap ("This guide covers install, config, and
deploy") helps the reader navigate. That's structure, not slop. See
[technical.md](technical.md).

## Telling instead of showing

Announcing difficulty or importance instead of demonstrating it.

- "This is genuinely hard"
- "This is what leadership actually looks like"
- "This actually matters"

Replace the announcement with the specific thing that makes it hard or important.

## Vague declaratives

Sentences that assert importance while naming nothing. The clearest sign of thin substance — fix
these in the substance pass, not the surface pass.

- "The reasons are structural"
- "The implications are significant"
- "This is the deepest problem"
- "The stakes are high"
- "The consequences are real"

If a sentence says something is important, deep, or structural without showing the specific thing,
either name the thing or cut the sentence.
